## Use your hardest ordinary order to compare all three

For a small online store, the choice depends on which implementation makes its actual orders, catalogue and customer communication manageable. Evaluate Shopify when the store drives the operation. Evaluate Wix when the business needs a website with products alongside services or bookings. Evaluate Squarespace when editorial, brand or portfolio pages have an important role in selling a smaller collection. These are starting points for a demonstration using your own products.

Choose a realistic order that includes a variant, a delivery rule and a possible change. Run the same approved test on each proposed setup. Compare the work your staff would do after payment and the bill under the same assumptions. A demonstration that ends at a successful card screen leaves important work unevaluated.

## Compare the actual plan and payment arrangement

Shopify's pricing page lists checkout, commerce features and plan-dependent payment rates. It also lists fees that may apply when using third-party payment providers. Check the terms for the region where the store will operate and the provider it intends to use. If the business sells in person, include the hardware, point-of-sale requirements and inventory workflow in the same evaluation. [Shopify pricing](https://www.shopify.com/pricing)

Wix's plan page lists payment acceptance and ecommerce on Core and higher tiers, with different commerce feature levels. Check the payment provider, specific checkout requirements and any needed apps. A mixed service-and-product business should also test the scheduling workflow if it will use one. [Wix plans](https://www.wix.com/plans)

Squarespace's pricing page lists selling features and distinguishes payment processing from certain online-store and digital-content transaction fees. Check the exact plan and product type. A physical product, a downloadable item and a membership can have different commercial requirements. [Squarespace pricing](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing)

Use current checkout quotes for your billing location. Store the date, currency, term, promotion expiry and included features alongside each quote so the business can compare them later.

## Write an order brief before building a homepage

Consider a hypothetical stationery studio. It sells notebooks in several finishes, boxed gift sets and products available for pickup. The owner packs orders and sells some of the same stock at events. A customer may buy a notebook online, collect it locally and ask to change the finish after ordering.

Use actual product data in your own evaluation. Pick one straightforward item, one with variants and one with an operational exception. Include real dimensions or packing requirements, the variants that share stock and the customer messages the team needs. Ask each supplier to explain how their proposed setup manages them.

### Check the catalogue after the first import

Create a small sample with realistic names, prices, photos and inventory records. Have a staff member edit a variant, add a new image and mark a product unavailable. Check what changes on the public page and in any linked system. Make sure the business can distinguish sold out, discontinued and temporarily hidden items.

Consider how the catalogue will be organized six months after launch. Record what an editor does to publish a new collection, retire old variants and link relevant products from content. A small store can start with a simple taxonomy and a repeatable product-page template. Add categories that help customers make decisions.

### Follow the order beyond payment

Use the payment provider's supported test approach. Verify cart calculations, delivery or pickup options, payment status, order record, stock behaviour, confirmation and the staff notification. Confirm that the packing team can identify the selected variant and any relevant delivery details without asking the customer again.

Run one exception as well. A product may be out of stock, a customer may select the wrong pickup option or the owner may need to cancel. Confirm which screen changes, what the customer sees and who owns any manual steps. Look at the order record after the test so you know whether any payment and inventory changes need reconciliation.

## Compare fees with the same assumptions

Build a worksheet with one column per platform. List the base subscription, domain, payment processing, platform fees if applicable, apps, themes, development, support and internal operating time. Use the same planned order volume and revenue for each column. Separate initial implementation, ordinary recurring expenses and costs that change with each order.

Use two scenarios based on your own plans. The first can reflect an ordinary trading period. The second can reflect a launch or seasonal period with a larger volume or more support. Include the features needed for both. Label the scenarios as budgeting assumptions and revise them when you have real sales data.

### Calculate a plan change using the actual fee difference

If a higher plan offers a lower percentage charge on eligible transactions, calculate the cost difference over the same period. For a hypothetical illustration, a higher plan costing 20 more per month and reducing an applicable fee by one percentage point would need 2,000 in eligible monthly sales to offset that difference. This assumes every eligible transaction receives the same reduction and the rest of the charges stay equal. Use the actual terms and your own payment mix for the decision.

Check which charges apply to refunds, different payment methods, cross-border payments and disputes where relevant. Ask for the source of each assumption. Pricing pages are a starting point, and the merchant agreement and live checkout terms determine the proposal you can accept.

## Give content and search work a place in the store

Plan what a customer needs to know about the products before buying. For a notebook, that could include size, paper, binding, how it looks in use and delivery timing. Add clear product photos and concise answers to genuine questions. Publish the store's actual delivery and returns information where a buyer can find it.

Ask the supplier to show the product and category page settings on the proposed build. Review titles, links between products and collections, visible information, mobile rendering and canonical URLs. Keep helpful editorial pages linked to relevant products. The [boutique ecommerce website guide](/insights/boutique-ecommerce-website-design-marketing) covers the surrounding marketing brief.

### Protect existing addresses during a move

List the current URLs customers use, including products, collections and editorial pages. Identify equivalent destinations and plan redirects. Confirm what happens to product variants, discontinued items, reviews, order history and customer accounts. Get a sample export before assuming every kind of record can move with its original function.

Test a representative group of old links after release. Check the final URL and the public page on a phone. Update internal links and the sitemap, then inspect key pages in Search Console. Use the [website migration checklist](/insights/seo-website-migration-checklist) to organize this work alongside checkout and inventory testing.

## Assign responsibility for a live store

Decide who handles new products, order exceptions, payment issues, access changes, promotions and regular checks. Ask the provider to document the release process for changes affecting checkout or a critical integration. A small store can keep the schedule simple if each recurring task has an owner.

Rehearse an unavailable supplier or staff absence. Can a second authorized person find the order, explain the status and follow the approved refund or cancellation process? Can they correct the public information after a holiday-hours change? These practical questions can reveal a support requirement that a feature list would miss.

## Choose the build your business can operate

Once the key workflows work, compare recurring cost and staff effort. A store-led business may value a proven order process and in-person stock handling. A service business adding products may value a coherent journey across enquiries, bookings and sales. An editorial brand may value the ability to publish strong product stories and maintain a modest catalogue. Ask each bidder to explain how their setup meets the exact brief.

## Test two stores with very different operating needs

### A local shop that shares stock across channels

Imagine a shop selling a limited run of products online and in person. The owner wants a buyer to reserve an item for pickup while a staff member can sell another one at a physical counter. The first task is to establish which system owns the stock count and how the other channels receive it. Ask each bidder to show the exact setup they propose, including any separate device, subscription or integration.

Give the evaluator a product with very low stock. Use supported testing to demonstrate what happens when a website order and a counter sale occur close together. Record the stock change in each system and check what the next customer sees. If the platform or integration handles conflicts with a manual step, assign the step to a person and estimate how often the business expects to use it. Agree how the owner will reconcile any discrepancy.

Now test pickup. Confirm that the customer can see when and where they should collect the order. Ask staff to show how they mark it ready and what happens when the buyer does not arrive. Review the wording and the order record after each step. Record the exact part of the solution that sends messages and what would happen if it failed.

### A service business that ships a few products

Imagine a photographer who takes booking enquiries and sells a small collection of prints. Ask the supplier to demonstrate a service enquiry and a separate product purchase. The service visitor should understand the enquiry process and when to expect a response. The product customer should be able to choose a size, see the delivery terms and complete a supported test order.

Check how both routes fit in the website navigation. Give the same brief to each bidder and ask the actual owner to find and edit the print listing and the booking instructions. Consider the cost of any separate scheduling, gallery or payment tools. An ecommerce plan may cover the sale while the business uses a different process for scheduling. Capture that difference in the quote and verify who owns each part.

Try a combined customer question. A buyer could ask whether a print can be collected during an appointment. The support team needs a clear way to find the order and explain the booking process, even if the two records are stored in different tools. Ask the vendor to identify which information can be transferred and where staff will handle exceptions manually.

## Ask each vendor for a comparable acceptance record

Use the same sheet for every demonstration. Include the test case, setup, observed result, open question and person who can resolve it. Keep a screenshot or a safe record reference when the result depends on a particular configuration. A successful demonstration should say which plan, account and region it used. If the live configuration will differ, ask for a check during handover.

Score the ordinary order first. It should pass through the storefront, payment, fulfilment and customer communication with the expected behaviour. Then score the hardest exception the business expects to handle regularly. If a return is likely to take several manual steps, make those steps visible in the price and training plan. A more expensive plan may be reasonable if the business can identify work it saves or a requirement it meets.

### Examine a return from the customer's side

Rehearse a scenario in which a buyer receives the wrong variant. Ask how the team identifies the order, records the problem and follows the store's approved return policy. Confirm what is refunded, what changes in inventory and what message goes to the customer. Use the supported test method and avoid generating a real charge merely for evaluation.

Review access as part of the test. The team may need a role that can find orders but requires another person's approval to issue a refund. Define that responsibility and verify it on the proposed setup. Include a process for correcting a mistake and deciding when a question belongs with the payment provider rather than the website supplier.

### Check which data moves during a migration

Ask the provider for a sample migration using permitted data. A product listing, its variants, images and collection relationships may need different handling. Check the output against the original and write down any gaps. For customer and order history, agree what needs to be retained, what belongs in the new system and what can remain in a secure archive.

Identify every public link the team uses in email, advertisements, packaging and social accounts. Choose how those links will reach the current product or an appropriate collection. Keep product pages that customers still rely on as useful destinations where possible. When a product is gone, make the destination and customer message a deliberate choice. Review the new sitemap after launch and track a representative set of addresses.

## Use the shortlist to decide what to build first

Separate requirements for opening the store from enhancements that depend on demonstrated demand. An opening scope might include a compact catalogue, one supported payment arrangement, clear delivery options and documented order handling. A later phase might add complex promotions, another market or more automation after staff know what they need.

Ask which work can be completed by the owner after launch. Price product photography, copy and initial inventory work separately when they are part of the brief. The owner should be able to tell which tasks the builder will handle and which will need time from staff. Use that schedule when comparing quotes with similar subscription fees.

Choose the proposal that meets the order and publishing requirements with an operating workload the business can afford. Keep the acceptance record so the team can check that the live store matches what it bought.

## Put a launch-day checklist in the handover

Have the owner and supplier agree which products and variants are approved for launch. Confirm the public prices, photos and relevant delivery information on the actual device a customer is likely to use. Check that each product card leads to the intended detail page. Review the return and contact information alongside checkout so a buyer can find help before or after paying.

Record the expected payment arrangement and how the team will verify it at release. Use the supported test procedure for the relevant provider. Ask staff to show where a new order appears, what information they need to fulfil it and how they record a cancellation. Confirm that the notification reaches the person who will actually pack or process orders. A named backup should be able to find and handle the same record.

Assign ownership for recurring charges and account access. Put the renewal dates and supplier contact information in the business's approved handover system. Note which apps or features affect checkout and stock. If the business decides to remove an integration later, ask the provider to describe the work that must be replaced and the checks to perform afterwards.

Finally, write down the evidence the owner wants after the first trading period. Examples include whether staff could find each order, which questions buyers asked about delivery and whether the current catalogue structure is easy to maintain. Separate problems with the website from issues with the offer or fulfilment process. The next round of work can then address observed customer and operational needs within the store's budget.
