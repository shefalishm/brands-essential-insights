The right website platform is the one your business can afford to operate and use for its actual customer journey. Wix, self-hosted WordPress and a custom website are different operating models, not three positions on a simple quality ladder. Decide who will edit the site, which functions are essential and who will support it before choosing.

A small clinic connecting an approved booking system has different needs from a consultant publishing service pages or a SaaS team showing a product workflow. Start with those tasks. A platform recommendation made without them is only a preference, not a scoped solution.

This guide offers a decision framework rather than a universal winner. It does not quote unverified subscription prices, claim a performance test we have not run or suggest that a particular tool guarantees enquiries.

## Understand the three approaches being compared

Wix is a hosted website platform. Its [hosting documentation](https://support.wix.com/en/article/exporting-or-embedding-your-wix-site-elsewhere) explains that sites depend on Wix infrastructure. This bundles important implementation choices into a managed product, while also creating constraints you should understand before a future move.

WordPress here means the open-source software associated with WordPress.org, not a comparison of WordPress.com subscription plans. Its [official features overview](https://wordpress.org/about/features/) describes publishing tools, user roles, themes and extensions. Your hosting, configuration and support arrangements still need to be chosen.

A custom website is not one standard product. It could be a small coded brochure site, a content-managed site with reusable templates or a complex application. Ask the developer to explain the architecture in plain language. The label alone does not establish cost, quality, portability or ease of editing.

A hybrid is possible, but it introduces another boundary to manage. Brands Essential's main site and this Insights hub use different systems. That is a description of this website, not evidence that every small business should copy the arrangement. Choose additional systems only when their purpose is clear.

## Write the non-negotiable customer journey first

List the actions the website must support. For a service business, that might be understanding a specific offer and requesting a discussion. For a cafe, it might be checking today's hours, reading a menu and reaching an ordering service. For software, it could be evaluating a workflow before requesting a demo.

Describe the successful path and the failure path. If an external booking tool is unavailable, what should a visitor do? If a form submission fails, is there a clear alternative? If a user returns later, can they find the same service without navigating through a campaign page?

Turn the list into requirements you can demonstrate. “Easy to manage” becomes “our office manager can change opening hours and preview the result.” “Connected to sales” becomes “an approved test enquiry reaches the responsible team with the correct service category.”

Use our [website development brief](/insights/website-development-brief) to record these tasks. A provider should recommend the platform after reviewing them, not retrofit your needs around its preferred implementation.

## When Wix is worth considering

Consider Wix when the agreed first release fits the platform's current capabilities and your team prefers a hosted editing environment. Ask for a demonstration using the actual page types and functions you need. Do not accept a showcase of unrelated templates as proof.

Check the specific plan, apps and integrations required. The [official Wix plan page](https://www.wix.com/plans) explains that billing and pricing can vary by location and subscription period. Record the terms relevant to your account rather than copying another business's subscription figure.

The main limitation to understand is architectural portability. A Wix site is not simply a folder you can move to any web host. Wix's documentation distinguishes its hosting dependence from your content ownership. Ask how content and URLs would be handled if you later changed platforms.

A hosted platform also does not eliminate editorial responsibility. Someone must still keep service descriptions accurate, review enquiries, manage permissions and approve images. Include those tasks in the operating plan rather than treating the subscription as a complete marketing service.

## When self-hosted WordPress is worth considering

Consider WordPress when its publishing structure and extension approach suit the requirements and there is an accountable maintenance arrangement. Review the editing experience with the people who will use it. A flexible system can still be impractical if ordinary tasks depend on someone unavailable.

The official [WordPress features page](https://wordpress.org/about/features/) documents roles, publishing capabilities and extensibility. This establishes available building blocks, not the quality of every theme, plugin or implementation. Assess the particular combination proposed for your project.

Ask who selects extensions, approves updates, checks compatibility, manages backups and restores the site after a failure. A managed host or provider may cover some of this, but read the service terms. Do not assume that buying hosting automatically buys all application maintenance.

Request a minimal extension inventory. For every addition, record its purpose, licence, renewal obligation and owner. Prefer a clear explanation of a necessary dependency to a long list presented as a measure of value.

## When custom development is worth considering

Consider custom development when essential requirements cannot be met sensibly with an existing approach, or when a deliberately small coded site fits your team's operating model. Require a clear account of what is custom and why. Bespoke work should solve a defined problem.

Ask how the website will be edited. A developer can build a visually simple page that still requires code changes for every update. If your team needs to publish frequently, include an appropriate content workflow in the scope rather than discovering the gap at handover.

Clarify repository ownership, hosting access, build instructions, external services and licences. Request a documented handover that another suitable provider could understand. Portability is a requirement to verify, not a benefit to assume from the word “custom.”

Have the provider separate application complexity from presentation preferences. A distinctive visual identity does not necessarily require bespoke ordering, booking or payment infrastructure. Reusing an appropriate established service may keep the website scope more manageable.

## Compare operating responsibilities instead of marketing labels

For each proposal, assign an owner to content edits, software changes, subscriptions, permission reviews, backups where relevant, incidents and integration failures. If a row has no owner, the operating model is incomplete regardless of platform.

Then ask who performs an ordinary update and who handles an unusual one. Changing a phone number may be straightforward, while adding a new service structure or connecting another CRM may require specialist work. Request examples that match your business, not a vague assurance of unlimited flexibility.

Define what happens when the original developer leaves. Can the business access its domain and platform? Are content assets and instructions available? Is there a support relationship or only informal goodwill? Put the answers in the agreement.

Our [website quote checklist](/insights/website-project-planner) helps separate the build, subscriptions and support. This distinction matters more to a constrained budget than deciding which platform has the largest feature list.

## Compare total ownership costs without inventing a winner

Create a cost record for initial work, recurring software, hosting or platform charges, content support and maintenance. Enter real supplier terms. Mark unknown costs explicitly rather than treating them as zero.

Ask which charges increase with usage, additional users or required features. Not every service uses the same billing model. The point is to inspect your actual contract, not assume that every subscription grows in the same way.

Account for migration and handover work when comparing an existing site with a rebuild. Reusing accurate content and a working structure may be worthwhile. Replacing technology solely because another provider prefers it needs a more convincing explanation.

A cheaper launch can still be appropriate. A more expensive proposal can still be unnecessary. Compare the smallest complete solution to the same customer problem, with transparent responsibilities, rather than assigning value based on technology prestige.

## Test integrations before making the platform decision final

List the systems the business already depends on: booking, ordering, email, CRM, payments or product accounts. Name the exact task for each connection. Determine whether the website needs a link, embed, data exchange or a completely custom workflow.

Ask the provider to show the planned handoff using vendor documentation or a test environment. Check the whole task: submission, acknowledgement, receiving team and failure recovery. A screenshot of a connected logo does not establish a functioning integration.

Review access and privacy needs separately. Adding a tool because it has a convenient integration does not establish that it is appropriate for sensitive information. For clinics, involve the right advisers before selecting form or tracking systems.

Use a short proof-of-fit exercise before committing to a complex feature. Define the question it must answer and what would cause you to reject the approach. This is more useful than discovering a fundamental constraint after the visual design is approved.

## SEO and AEO do not choose the platform for you

Ask whether the delivered site has useful crawlable content, appropriate titles and canonical URLs, internal links and a usable mobile experience. When replacing a site, include redirect planning. These are requirements to verify in the implementation.

Google's [AI features documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) says established SEO practices remain relevant for its AI search features. It does not require special AI markup. A developer's choice of framework is not proof of eligibility, citations or rankings.

For answer-oriented writing, explain the core decision directly and support it with specific details. Platform comparison content should tell a reader what changes the recommendation, not repeat that every option is flexible and powerful.

If a supplier sells “SEO included,” ask what will actually be delivered and reviewed. Technical configuration and ongoing editorial work are different activities. Neither is a promise of first place in search results.

## A decision exercise for three small teams

For a solo consultant, start with accurate service information, genuine proof and a reliable enquiry route. Test whether the proposed editing arrangement lets the consultant maintain those basics. Defer a complex publishing system if there is no realistic content process behind it.

For a cafe, test the menu and hours update process and the ordering handoff. The right implementation should fit how staff work during service. Do not choose software around a presentation of features that nobody can operate.

For a SaaS team, decide how marketing pages relate to the application. A product login, trial and demonstration may have different owners and release processes. Avoid making every marketing copy change depend on a product deployment without discussing the consequence.

These are planning exercises, not client case studies or claims that one platform must serve each industry. The recommendation can change when requirements, staffing or existing investments change.

## Ask to perform a real edit before sign-off

Have the nominated operator change a piece of business information, replace an approved image and preview a page. Observe where they need assistance. Document the task and any permissions needed to perform it safely.

Test publishing and recovery. Can an accidental change be corrected through the agreed workflow? Who approves content before it goes live? What is the escalation route if an integration stops working?

Include mobile checking in the exercise. A page can look correct in a wide desktop preview while a long heading or embedded tool becomes awkward on a phone. Test the actual content, not only a sample screen.

If the operator needs training, scope it. If the business prefers not to edit at all, scope an ongoing service. Neither choice is inherently wrong. The problem is buying one arrangement while assuming the other.

## Use a platform decision record before approving the build

Write a short decision record rather than keeping the choice inside a supplier presentation. Begin with the customer journey and the people who will operate the site. State which requirements are essential for the first release and which are explicitly deferred.

Next, record the proposed architecture in ordinary language. Name the platform, hosting arrangement, editing interface and connected systems. Identify which supplier supports each part. A business owner should be able to explain where an enquiry goes without understanding every technical implementation detail.

Add a row for each unresolved question. Examples include whether the existing booking account permits the proposed handoff, whether an editor can maintain a new page type and whether a required licence transfers to the business. Assign an owner and do not mark the question resolved without evidence.

Record why the selected approach fits better than the alternatives for this release. A valid reason might be a demonstrated workflow or a maintenance arrangement the team can support. “The developer likes it” is incomplete unless the business has considered the operational consequences.

Include the disadvantages you are accepting. A hosted platform can impose migration constraints; an extensible publishing system needs an explicit maintenance plan; custom work needs a clear editing and handover arrangement. Naming a limitation does not automatically disqualify the choice. It makes the trade-off deliberate.

Ask what would cause a review later. A new integration, a different publishing team or a changed business model may justify reassessing the arrangement. Avoid treating an uncertain future requirement as a reason to build everything now.

Before final approval, have the actual operator repeat the essential task in the proposed environment. If they cannot perform it with the planned permissions and training, revise the workflow or support scope. Do not substitute a specialist's quick demonstration for the business's own ability to operate the result.

Keep the record with the project handover. It gives a future supplier context about why decisions were made, what was tested and which limitations were knowingly accepted. That is more useful than a universal platform ranking that ignores the circumstances of the project.
