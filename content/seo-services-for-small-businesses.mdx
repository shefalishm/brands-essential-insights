## Buy a defined outcome for the first engagement

The right first SEO service helps a small business answer three questions. Can Google reach the important pages? Do those pages explain the offer clearly? Can the business handle and measure the enquiries they generate? Pay for a scope that names the pages, the work and the person who will implement it.

If you need an investigation, commission a focused audit with an implementation plan. If the issues are known, a fixed project can fund the repairs and verification directly. Consider a recurring agreement when you can identify the decisions and tasks someone will own each month. Google recommends asking prospective suppliers about their process and avoiding first-place guarantees. [Google's guidance on hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo)

## Give every bidder the same brief

Name the service that needs attention, where you can deliver it and what a useful enquiry looks like. Include the website URL, the person who approves changes and any constraints. For example, an independent studio might need more enquiries for weekday classes and have limited capacity for private events. A proposal focused entirely on event traffic would miss the business objective.

Give bidders representative pages and ask them to explain what they would review first. A practical answer can identify how they will examine search access, confirm customer questions and trace the form or booking route. Ask for the evidence they need from your team and the work they expect your developer to do.

A useful proposal identifies where it would change the website. Ask for a sample deliverable with an affected page, proposed change, reason, owner and acceptance check. A finding such as “improve metadata” leaves several decisions open. A scoped task might identify a specific service page whose title describes the company but omits the service. The owner can approve the replacement, and the developer can confirm the published result.

## Separate the audit from the implementation

Ask the supplier to price diagnosis, implementation and follow-up as distinct pieces of work. A technical review may find unintended indexing settings or links leading to redirected pages. The quote should say who changes those settings and verifies the live site. A content review may recommend revising an offer, but the business still has to confirm its scope and availability.

If an audit has a long list of recommendations, ask which ones affect the priority customer journey. Give urgent problems a short route to a decision. A broken booking link on a commercially important page can be verified and repaired before commissioning a new set of informational articles. Record evidence before and after the change.

## Use a proposed 90-day scope

### Month one. Establish the baseline

Choose one or two priority services. Check representative URLs and record their current search status. Confirm the main enquiry route with an authorised test and ask staff to explain how they recognise a suitable lead. Record the baseline period and relevant changes such as a new location or reduced opening hours. [Search Console indexing guide](/insights/how-to-get-website-indexed-by-google)

### Month two. Improve the buyer journey

Publish the agreed service-page revisions and check the results on a phone. Give each page clear scope, relevant proof and a next step. Add links from pages that already help customers choose a service. For an eligible local business, align public hours, services and the profile destination with the approved website information. [Local SEO plan](/insights/local-seo-for-small-business)

### Month three. Review activity and make a decision

Compare like periods and identify changes in visibility, visits and eligible enquiries. Look at the underlying queries and enquiries before deciding what to do. A rise in visits from people outside the service area may call for a clearer coverage explanation. A rise in qualified requests that receive no response points to a follow-up problem the business can address.

## Ask how the retainer earns its place

Request the recurring deliverables, how exceptions are handled, the cost of additional development and who owns the accounts. Ask what information stays available after the engagement ends. Agree which results support renewal and what work a smaller budget could still cover. Keep recurring care such as form checks or renewals visible in the quote. ## Score proposals against the same evidence

Ask each bidder to return the same four items. Have them identify the first customer journey they would improve, name the access they need to investigate it, describe a change the business could approve, and explain what the owner will see when the work is complete. Score each item as clear, incomplete or unresolved. Record the reason alongside the score.

For a hypothetical cabinetmaker, the first journey could be a homeowner searching for a fitted storage service, reviewing examples and requesting an estimate. An agency might recommend clarifying the types of projects it accepts and updating the form to ask for dimensions and a service area. Another might propose publishing a series of broader home improvement articles. Ask both to explain how the work supports the specific enquiry you need. The owner can then choose whether the most pressing issue is reach, clarity or follow-up.

### Give the team a handover it can use

Keep a business-owned record of accounts, approved page copy, published changes and open issues. Use role-based access where the relevant service supports it. Give the supplier the access necessary for the work and identify who at the business can approve public changes. If an agency is diagnosing a problem, ask what it can learn with read access before granting editing rights.

Put the current target pages and decisions in the same place as the reporting notes. If the supplier changes, the next person should be able to tell why a page was revised, which customer question it answers and which checks are still due. This is particularly useful when the business has only one person responsible for marketing and website operations.

### Choose a reporting question before choosing a chart

Compare observations in a sequence. Start with whether the planned changes were completed. Then inspect relevant impressions and clicks when available. Finally, ask the person who receives enquiries what customers requested and how many fit the service. A clear result might be that the new page now explains project minimums and staff report fewer unsuitable requests. Record the sample and time period rather than generalising from a handful of calls.

If the owner has little lead data, keep the initial tracking simple. Ask staff to record the service requested, whether the request fits and whether it was answered. A test enquiry should appear in the right place and be distinguishable from a real request. Use unknown for any source the business cannot establish. The review should end with a next decision, such as clarifying a specific service or fixing a response process, and name who will make it.
