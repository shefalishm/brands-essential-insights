An affordable website is not simply the proposal with the lowest total. It is a website whose build cost, running cost and operating demands fit the business. Before choosing a website design company, agree what the site must help a customer do, what the provider will deliver and what you will be responsible for after launch.

This guide does not invent a market-average price or advertise an unverified Brands Essential package. It gives you a way to compare real quotes, identify missing work and request a smaller first release. The checklists are our recommended buying process, not claims about every agency's contract.

## Start with the job, not the page count

Write a sentence describing the first useful outcome: “A prospective customer should understand our service, confirm we are suitable and send an enquiry that reaches the correct person.” Replace that sentence if you need appointments, orders or demonstrations. This becomes the boundary of the first release.

A page count alone is a weak specification. A contact page might contain a simple email link or a form connected to a CRM with routing and confirmation rules. A services page might describe one offer or require multiple reusable layouts. Ask what each page does and what happens after an action.

Create a page inventory with a purpose and an owner. Identify the visitor question, primary action, source material and approver for each page. If nobody can explain why a page is needed, consider deferring it. Our [website development brief](/insights/website-development-brief) provides a useful starting point.

## Separate the build, subscriptions and ongoing labour

Ask for three clearly labelled groups. Project work includes discovery, structure, writing, design, development, testing and launch. Third-party spending includes hosting, domain registration, licensed assets and connected services. Ongoing labour includes updates, content changes, reporting and agreed marketing work.

Do not let one monthly figure hide the distinctions. Ask which charges continue after the build, which are optional and what happens if you stop the relationship. Read the contract to understand whether payments buy a completed asset, continued access to a service, or a combination.

For subscriptions, record the account holder, billing period, renewal basis and cancellation process. Identify dependencies. A booking tool may be essential while an animation extension is optional. The list helps you reduce discretionary spending without interrupting the main customer task.

Wix's [official plan page](https://www.wix.com/plans) notes that prices and currency vary by location. Check current purchase terms rather than relying on a price copied from an old comparison article. This guide deliberately avoids quoting a subscription amount that may not apply to your location or billing period.

## Clarify strategy and content responsibilities

“Website design included” does not establish whether anyone will help clarify your offer. Ask whether the provider is organising supplied information or researching your audience and structuring your services. Both can be reasonable, but they represent different deliverables.

Identify who drafts each page, supplies service facts, verifies qualifications and approves claims. Confirm whether interviews, copy editing, image selection and content entry are included. A project cannot be ready to build if essential material has no assigned producer.

Review a representative page before every page is populated. It should answer an actual customer question, use approved business information and provide an understandable next step. A long article or attractive template is not a substitute for information specific to your business.

Request image sources and permitted uses. Illustrative AI images should not masquerade as actual staff, premises or customers. Where real people or physical locations are part of the offer, decide what genuine, approved material the business can supply.

## Make design and development deliverables testable

Ask how many unique layouts are included. Several service pages may reuse one template, while a calculator introduces different behaviour and testing. Identify which pages use shared layouts, which need custom work and who can create additional pages after handover.

Translate “responsive” into acceptance checks: readable text, usable menus, working forms and clear feedback when an action fails. Ask how keyboard navigation and visible focus will be reviewed. Clarify whether a specialist accessibility assessment is included or requires a separate engagement.

Name integrations and their expected handoffs. “CRM integration” is incomplete without identifying the fields, destination, recipient and failure handling. Distinguish a simple external link, an embedded vendor tool and a custom connection between systems.

Ask whether configuration and testing are included alongside the visual interface. A developer may price an appointment button without setting up the booking account. The quote should describe the complete customer task and any work another provider must perform.

## Compare proposals against the same checklist

Give providers the same brief. Mark every item as included, excluded, supplied by you or requiring clarification. Do not score an ambiguous item as included because it appeared in a sales presentation.

- Business goal, audience and priority conversion.
- Page inventory and reusable templates.
- Writing, imagery and factual approvals.
- Mobile navigation, form behaviour and failure messages.
- Integrations, access requirements and receiving systems.
- Search migration if replacing an existing site.
- Account ownership, licences and handover.
- Testing, corrections and launch responsibilities.
- Recurring subscriptions and optional support.
- A process for approving out-of-scope charges.

When a quote is lower, ask which items differ. You may discover a simpler appropriate solution. You may also discover that you must write the copy, populate the pages or manage redirects yourself. Neither is automatically unacceptable, but each changes the comparison.

## Build a total-cost worksheet using real quotes

Create rows for the build, domain, hosting or platform, email, booking tools, paid extensions, maintenance, content work and migration. Enter verified quotes or label a row “not yet quoted.” Do not insert an optimistic figure just to make the budget balance.

For each item, record the supplier, payment frequency, mandatory or optional status and cancellation consequences. Separately identify the time your team must contribute: preparing photographs, reviewing copy, checking services and answering incoming enquiries.

Calculate launch spending separately from operating commitments. For your chosen comparison period, add the payments actually due and agreed support. Keep optional marketing experiments separate so you can see the cost of running the site before deciding how to attract visitors.

This is a procurement worksheet, not a return forecast. A website quotation does not establish future sales. Use your own commercial evidence when assessing viability, and keep confirmed costs distinct from assumptions about visitor demand or enquiry quality.

## Reduce scope without cutting the working customer journey

Remove features that do not support the first useful task. A consultant may need a clear offer and enquiry route before a resource library. A cafe may need a readable menu before custom ordering software. A SaaS founder may need a demonstration page before a large publishing system.

Prefer reusable layouts for similar information. Prepare approved copy and images. Nominate one person to consolidate feedback. These are scope-control recommendations, not promises that every provider will reduce a quote by a particular amount.

Ask for phased options. The first release should be a complete useful journey, not an unfinished fragment. Document what later work will reuse, what may need replacing and which early decisions could create future constraints.

Protect functional contact routes, appropriate access controls, necessary privacy review and launch testing. When the budget is tight, deferring an optional effect is more sensible than accepting an untested form. Read [what makes a website convert](/insights/what-makes-a-website-convert) for a related evaluation framework.

## Check ownership and the exit path

Identify the domain registrant and administrators of the platform, hosting and connected services. Request business-controlled accounts with appropriate collaborator access. Paying an invoice does not by itself establish who controls each asset or account.

For custom work, ask which source files, repositories and documentation transfer at handover. Separate commissioned assets from software licensed for use. Confirm any restrictions on fonts, imagery, themes and extensions before assuming you can reuse them elsewhere.

Platform portability matters too. Wix explains that its sites need to run on [Wix's infrastructure](https://support.wix.com/en/article/exporting-or-embedding-your-wix-site-elsewhere). That is different from copying a self-hosted codebase to another server. Understand the architecture rather than describing every subscription as the same thing.

If you are comparing approaches, read our [Wix, WordPress and custom website guide](/insights/wix-wordpress-custom-website-small-business). The relevant question is who will operate the site and what it must do, not which platform wins every possible comparison.

## Define maintenance in plain language

Ask for a list of activities and their triggers. Separate infrastructure tasks from content editing. Clarify responsibility for updates, backup and restoration arrangements where applicable, broken forms, renewals and changes to connected services.

Agree what the arrangement excludes. A new service page or replacement integration may be an enhancement, not maintenance. Request an approval process for additional work before anyone begins charging for it.

Have the provider describe an incident path: where you report a problem, who assesses it and what contractual response terms mean. “Support included” does not establish availability, response time or resolution time without further explanation.

Request a practical handover demonstration. Your nominated operator should be able to correct business hours, edit a service description and verify that enquiries reach the right place. Documentation should reflect the delivered website, not an unrelated generic tutorial.

## Treat launch and migration as work, not a switch

If replacing a site, list important existing URLs before changing them. Decide which pages remain, move or retire. Ask who implements and verifies redirects. Our [website migration guide](/insights/seo-website-migration-checklist) can help frame this part of the brief.

Define published-environment checks: domain resolution, secure page loading, canonical URLs, sitemap access, indexing instructions, forms and connected services. A successful preview submission is not enough to establish that production enquiries reach the business.

Distinguish a contact click from a completed enquiry. Ask what measurement will record, how consent is handled and who reviews results. Do not transmit message bodies or unnecessary personal details to analytics just because the form makes them available.

Search deliverables should be concrete: useful text, accurate titles, crawlable pages and relevant links. Avoid unsupported guarantees of first place or AI citations. Google's [AI search documentation](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/appearance/ai-features) explains that normal SEO foundations remain relevant and special markup is not required.

## Organise approvals and payments around identifiable outputs

Define milestones such as an approved brief, content structure, representative page and tested launch. The payment arrangement belongs in the agreement, but each stage should mean more than a date passing.

Define revision rounds. Does a round cover consolidated feedback on an agreed direction or a complete redesign? Record how scope changes are flagged and authorised. Extra charges should not depend on two people remembering a conversation differently.

Check dependencies before agreeing a launch date. Identify when the business supplies content, photographs, access and approval. If an input is delayed or changes, discuss the effect on the plan explicitly.

Keep a short decision log with the requested change, reason, cost implication and owner. It helps the team make deliberate trade-offs rather than allow new requests to accumulate invisibly.

## A practical acceptance exercise before final sign-off

Ask someone unfamiliar with the project to start on a service page and explain what the business does. Can they identify who the service is for, what is included, what evidence supports the offer and how to ask a question? Record confusion rather than telling them what they should have noticed.

Then test the action. Submit an appropriate non-sensitive test enquiry, confirm it arrives, check the acknowledgement and verify that someone is responsible for replying. Test an incomplete submission and an unavailable external service as well as the successful path.

Review ownership with the person who will maintain the site. Confirm that they can access the required accounts without using a departing supplier's personal login. Record support contacts and any subscriptions they must renew.

Separate defects from new ideas. A broken agreed form is not the same as a new booking feature requested at sign-off. A documented acceptance process helps you fix the delivered scope while planning improvements in a controlled way.

## Resolve a quote discrepancy before signing

When proposals describe different solutions, ask each provider to restate the same minimum release. Request the price for that agreed scope, then list optional additions separately. This avoids comparing a content-led redesign with a template installation as if they were the same purchase.

Use specific questions. If one provider includes copywriting and another does not, ask what material the second expects and whether it will review that material before design. If one includes migration, ask for the URL mapping and redirect responsibilities. If support differs, ask which incidents and changes each arrangement covers.

Request a written clarification rather than relying on a reassuring call. A short answer attached to the proposal is easier to check at handover than a memory of what the salesperson intended. Give the provider an opportunity to correct misunderstandings before assuming that an omission is deliberate.

Watch for incompatible assumptions. One quote may assume you already have approved branding; another may include identity work. One may assume a functioning booking account; another may include configuration. Confirm the starting conditions and decide which work the business actually needs.

Then compare the proposed acceptance criteria. If one provider promises a launch and another promises a tested enquiry journey with training, ask both what evidence demonstrates completion. Clear acceptance makes a smaller engagement easier to evaluate and prevents an attractive presentation from becoming the only measure of delivery.

Keep negotiation focused on choices. Ask what can be simplified, reused or deferred, and what consequence follows. Do not ask a provider to remove the cost of necessary work while quietly expecting it to remain included. A transparent smaller scope is easier to manage than an underfunded version of the original plan.

Finally, check the final document against the original brief. Confirm that the visitor's main task still works from beginning to end and that your own responsibilities are realistic. If the plan depends on content or access you cannot supply, resolve that dependency before agreeing a start.
