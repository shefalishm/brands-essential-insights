## Squarespace or Webflow: choose the publishing operation, not just the homepage

A useful Squarespace vs Webflow comparison starts with who will publish and maintain the website. For a small business, the right implementation should make ordinary work manageable: changing an offer, adding a service, publishing a useful article and answering an enquiry. Compare both around those tasks before deciding.

Our editorial recommendation is to give owner editing and content structure the same attention as design. A website that looks suitable at launch can still require more specialist help than its owner expected. Conversely, a more involved implementation may be justified when it solves a specific publishing requirement and someone is responsible for maintaining it.

We have not performed a usability study, speed comparison or conversion experiment. The exercises below are proposed evaluation methods. Regional pricing and billing choices require confirmation in your own checkout.

## The verified distinctions worth taking into a proposal

[Squarespace's pricing page](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) lists Basic, Core, Plus and Advanced website plans. Its [plan-selection guidance](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/206536797-Choose-the-right-Squarespace-plan) explains that website and other Squarespace subscriptions are billed separately. Ask which services the proposed quote actually includes.

[Webflow's pricing page](https://webflow.com/pricing) currently distinguishes Site plans from other plan types. It describes Basic for sites without a CMS and Premium for content-rich sites with CMS requirements. Do not assume the plan names in an older comparison still describe the current offer.

Webflow's [official code-export documentation](https://help.webflow.com/hc/en-us/articles/33961386739347-How-do-I-export-my-Webflow-site-code) says export requires a paid Workspace plan; Site plans alone do not include it. The export excludes CMS functionality and other hosted functions, including form processing and site search. That distinction matters if portability is part of the sales proposal.

These are documented product facts. They do not establish that one platform is easier for your employee, better for every design or more effective at generating business. Those judgements need a specific implementation and evaluation.

## Write the growth requirement in concrete terms

“A website that can grow” is too vague to price or evaluate. Replace it with the changes you reasonably expect to make. Perhaps a consultancy wants to add a new service and publish research. Perhaps a small SaaS company needs product explanations, documentation links and a demo request. Perhaps a local studio needs recurring workshop announcements.

These are hypothetical briefs, not client results. For each, write the content objects the website needs: service, article, team member, event or product explanation. Identify what information repeats and what needs an individual layout. Do not create a complicated content model solely because the software offers one.

List the people involved. A founder may approve claims, a marketer may write and a designer may maintain layout components. If the same person performs every role, the workflow still needs to be understandable. A system designed for several specialists may be unnecessarily demanding for an owner who updates the site occasionally.

### Define what a successful ordinary week looks like

Use a week as a planning frame, not a promised delivery schedule. Describe the publishing tasks your business wants to complete during it. One business might need to revise a service paragraph and publish an article. Another might need to update several reusable product explanations while keeping an old campaign page unchanged.

Ask the builder to explain how that work happens in the proposed setup. This conversation is more revealing than an abstract claim that a platform is scalable. Growth should describe a manageable change, an owner and the resources it requires.

## CMS design: decide what should be structured

A content management system is useful when the website needs a repeatable way to store and publish content. The buying question is what structure your business needs, not how many fields you can add. Begin with one real piece of content and identify the information an editor must supply.

For an article, that might include a title, short description, publication date, image, body and related service. For a service page, it might include the intended customer, deliverables, limitations and next step. Keep fields tied to a clear publishing purpose. Unused fields become another decision for the editor to misunderstand or ignore.

Ask to see content with awkward but realistic variations. A long headline, a missing optional image or a detailed service limitation should not require the team to improvise a new design. Demonstrate what happens when content is incomplete and how the editor knows it is ready for approval.

### Separate content reuse from duplication

If the same service description appears in several places, identify the authoritative version and how the implementation keeps it consistent. If the content should differ by context, explain the difference. A homepage summary and a detailed service page do not have to contain identical paragraphs.

Do not publish extra pages simply because the system makes it easy. Each page should answer a distinct customer need. When two proposed pages cover the same question, consider combining them before creating more editing and maintenance work.

## Owner editing is an acceptance test

Invite the actual editor to the demonstration. Give them the approved copy and ask them to complete a task while the builder observes. Avoid accepting a demonstration performed only by the person who assembled the site. Their familiarity can hide decisions that a new editor finds confusing.

Record whether the editor can find the correct content, understand what is safe to change, preview the result and finish the agreed publication process. Ask what they would do if a layout looks wrong. A clear boundary between content editing and design changes can be helpful when everyone understands it.

Treat confusion as a design input. It may require clearer labels, a simpler component, better training or a change in responsibilities. Do not automatically interpret every difficulty as a fundamental platform problem. Equally, do not buy an ongoing specialist dependency while expecting a completely self-managed website.

### Specify the handover materials

Request instructions for the tasks the owner will repeat, plus a record of account ownership and renewal responsibilities. Include where approved images live, how new requests are submitted and which changes need review. A short, accurate guide for this website is more useful than an enormous generic manual.

Ask the owner to repeat the task using the guide before sign-off. This checks the handover itself. It is a proposed project acceptance method, not a claim that either vendor guarantees an effortless learning process.

## Design flexibility needs an operating budget

Before approving elaborate animation or unusual page behaviour, ask what commercial purpose it serves. Does it explain a difficult product, guide the visitor or demonstrate the brand's work? If its role is mainly decorative, make sure the business can afford the additional design and maintenance effort.

Request a reduced-motion and small-screen review as part of the design discussion where relevant. Examine whether the page remains understandable without relying on motion. Ask how the implementation handles unusually long text and different image shapes. These are requirements for your proposed build, not a benchmark result for either platform.

A small business can still have a distinctive website. Distinction can come from useful explanations, thoughtful photography, clear typography and a coherent page system. A large number of individually designed sections is not the only route to a recognisable brand.

### Build around representative content

Provide the real service description before judging the layout. A design made around a short placeholder sentence may not accommodate a complicated offer well. Ask for the most important page to be demonstrated with content that is close to final.

Agree how future pages will use the system. The cost of a new service page should be understandable: content work, layout assembly, review and any additional implementation. This helps the owner judge what “growth” will cost after the initial build.

## Compare total ownership cost without a misleading price race

Create separate rows for the website subscription, any required Workspace arrangement, domains, business email, integrations and ongoing support. Record the billing term and renewal expectation for each. Only include a service when the proposed workflow needs it.

Then add the project work: discovery, information structure, copy, design, build, migration, launch checks and training. Two proposals are not equivalent if one includes original service-page writing and the other assumes the owner will supply everything. Identify those differences before comparing totals.

Request a quote for your billing location rather than treating a currency shown during research as a US price. Avoid substituting a monthly equivalent for the actual payment due. If a feature requires a separate plan, include that commitment explicitly.

### Price the work that follows publishing

Someone needs to check enquiries, correct outdated claims, supply new information and decide which improvements deserve attention. List these responsibilities even if no external retainer is planned. If you buy support, describe it in actions and exclusions.

For example, “monthly support” should explain whether it covers a form fault, a new article, a layout revision or only advice. Ask who responds when an external service changes its behaviour. Use our [website project planner](/insights/website-project-planner) to separate subscription expenses from the work of running the website.

## SEO and AEO: ask for page-level evidence

Do not buy a ranking promise attached to a platform name. Ask the supplier to explain the proposed URL structure, page titles, headings, internal links and published service information. Request a launch record showing the agreed checks and any unresolved issues.

Inspect the most important page as a potential customer. Does it say who the service is for, what is included, what affects scope and how to start? Are claimed results supported and limitations clear? These questions give your content review a concrete purpose.

For AEO, keep the brief specific. Ask for useful answers grounded in the actual service, clear business information and an implementation review. A special label on a package does not establish inclusion in an AI response. Our [small-business AEO checklist](/insights/answer-engine-optimization-small-business-checklist) covers a task-based service-page audit.

### Keep conversion work connected to the page

Review the enquiry route alongside the content. A visitor who has understood the offer should know what to do next and what information to provide. Ask for confirmation messages and follow-up ownership as part of the acceptance process.

If you intend to run campaigns, record which page each campaign will use and what customer action it should support. Do not assume that adding more traffic will resolve an unclear offer or an unanswered inbox.

## Portability: distinguish an export from an operating website

The Webflow export distinction deserves a direct conversation. Exported files and an equivalent hosted service are different deliverables. If a proposal promises independence through code export, ask the supplier to list every function the exported version would need to replace.

Use the same discipline for either platform: inventory copy, images, URLs, content records, forms, bookings, third-party connections and ownership. Obtain a written answer describing what can move and what must be rebuilt for the exact setup. A general statement that data can be exported is not a migration plan.

Keep your approved content and asset permissions in business-owned storage. Record important URLs and connected services as the site develops. This reduces the amount of information you would have to reconstruct under time pressure, without assuming any future move will be automatic.

### Ask about the exit process before signing

Who can transfer account access? What happens to subscriptions when the supplier relationship ends? Where will the business obtain the latest approved copy? How will a replacement supplier understand the implementation? These are ordinary ownership questions and should not require an emergency to answer.

A clear exit arrangement can support a healthy ongoing relationship. It lets the business buy continuing help because that help is useful, with a shared understanding of what the owner controls.

## A small-business decision workshop

Start with one representative service page and one representative content item. Give the same materials to each proposed implementation. Evaluate the actual publishing and enquiry tasks, then record what is demonstrated, what is documented and what remains uncertain.

Make essential requirements pass-or-fail before applying preferences. If the intended editor cannot complete a necessary task and no affordable support arrangement exists, that is an operating gap. A visually impressive demo elsewhere should not conceal it.

Once both proposals meet the essentials, compare the remaining commitments: design maintenance, publication responsibility, recurring costs and future change work. Use an explicit reason for the final choice. “Our owner can maintain this agreed scope with this support arrangement” is more actionable than “the internet says it is best.”

Create a later-phase list for optional features. Give each a trigger, such as a real content workflow or customer requirement. That preserves ambition without purchasing an operating system larger than the business can support today.

### Keep a launch issue list with decisions

During review, place each issue in a shared list with the affected page, the observed problem and the person responsible for resolving it. Distinguish a broken requirement from a new request. A missing confirmation message may be unfinished agreed work; an additional service page may be a scope change. Agree that distinction against the brief rather than negotiating it from memory.

Before launch, record which issues are resolved, which are accepted with an explanation and which prevent release. Give the owner a readable version of the record. This makes the final approval about the website's actual state and the agreed customer journey. It also gives future support a useful starting point when someone asks why a particular decision was made.
