## Start with the service customers can actually buy

A practical local SEO plan connects an eligible Google Business Profile, a useful website and the team that answers enquiries. In the first month, pick one valuable service and follow the journey a customer takes from a local search to a call, quote or booking. Fix gaps in that route before expanding coverage.

Google describes local ranking in terms of relevance, distance and prominence. Complete business information helps explain what you offer, while distance depends on the searcher and the business location. Use that distinction when evaluating a supplier who presents a single Maps screenshot as a result. [Google's local ranking guidance](https://support.google.com/business/answer/7091)

This is a proposed 30-day work plan. The workshop examples are hypothetical and illustrate implementation decisions.

## Days 1 to 5. Map the services and areas you can serve

Open a sheet and create a row for each main service. Add the customer problem, geography, booking method, constraints and the person responsible. Ask the team which enquiries they can handle, which routinely fall outside the brief and why. Capture questions in the customer's own words without including names or personal details.

Consider a bicycle repair shop. A customer asking about a puncture may want to know whether they can walk in. Someone with an electric bicycle may need to know whether the shop handles their system. A family arranging several tune-ups may need an estimate and a drop-off time. Each question describes a different decision even if the enquiry ends up with the same mechanic.

Set the scope explicitly. The shop could prioritize its tune-up service, use a single regional page for customers who travel to the workshop and address walk-in rules on that page. A mobile mechanic offering scheduled visits would need different coverage information. They might describe where they travel, how bookings are grouped and when customers should contact them about a location beyond the regular route.

### Record the proof behind each location claim

Use verifiable information such as an actual address, approved service coverage, genuine work examples and directions that staff can confirm. For a storefront, record entrance details and opening hours. For a service-area business, record where the team travels and how customers should check availability. Ask the owner to approve these facts before writing.

A town name in a search query is a useful clue about what a customer needs. A page with the same text as another page and a different town in the title leaves that need unanswered. If a town changes the travel process, turnaround, collection option or location contact, describe the difference. If customers receive the same service across a region, a single page can make the coverage easier to maintain.

## Days 6 to 10. Connect the profile and website

Find the existing Business Profile and confirm who owns it. Review the customer-facing name, primary activity, hours, contact number and website destination against the approved facts. Follow Google's eligibility and representation rules before editing locations or categories. Online-only businesses have a different discovery route through their websites and other channels. [Google Business Profile eligibility](https://support.google.com/business/answer/13763036) and [business representation guidelines](https://support.google.com/business/answer/3038177)

Follow the actual profile website link on a phone. The visitor should arrive at a page that explains the relevant business and shows a working next step. If the site has several stores, check the correct location's hours and phone number. If a service business uses a central homepage, confirm someone can reach the relevant service and enquiry route without a long menu search.

For detailed profile settings and performance definitions, use the [Google Business Profile optimization guide](/insights/google-business-profile-optimization-small-business).

### Fix contradictions before adding new material

Make a short change list when opening hours, services or locations disagree across the website, profile and booking system. Establish the approved fact and assign responsibility for each update. Check the public version after saving. A common operational issue is a business updating its homepage and forgetting the booking confirmation, which continues to send an old address.

Use the same process for a temporary change. Record the date the change starts and ends, the pages it affects and who will review it afterwards. Confirm it against the reality customers encounter rather than relying on the latest draft in a shared folder.

## Days 11 to 19. Improve the service page people are looking for

Write a page that answers the decision behind the search. For the hypothetical shop, the tune-up page should name the service, explain which bicycles it accepts, describe the work the team actually includes and tell people how to book. If the mechanic needs to assess the bicycle first, explain that in the price or estimate section.

Start with an accurate page title and a direct opening. Follow it with scope, common limitations, practical examples and a visible call or booking route. Link from the relevant homepage or services list. Offer a short FAQ where customers need clarification and the answer can be maintained. For a related build worksheet, see the [local service landing-page blueprint](/insights/local-landing-page-blueprint).

### Give each page a defined customer decision

A homepage helps the customer recognize the business and choose a route. A service page explains the offer and helps the customer decide whether to enquire. A legitimate location page answers local questions such as where to go, whether that branch offers the service and how to book there. Those roles can coexist without forcing every page to repeat the same offer.

Take a sample enquiry that staff would consider a good fit. Ask a colleague who has never seen the site to use the page to determine whether the business can help. Give them only the questions a customer would have. Record where they pause or use the wrong route. Clarify that part of the page before adding a new marketing section.

### Show work people can verify

Choose genuine examples with approval to publish. A repair business could show the kind of equipment it handles and explain the steps a customer goes through. A service company could explain a real process without disclosing the customer's address. Clear photographs of the actual business are useful where they help people identify a storefront or understand the work.

Use the same care with reviews. An operating process should cover when the team requests honest feedback, who reads it and what needs a private response. Repeated questions about opening hours or deposits can point to missing page information. Discuss the underlying confusion with staff before rewriting a public response.

## Days 20 to 25. Test enquiry handling before seeking more visits

Make an authorized test enquiry from the service page on a phone. Confirm what the customer sees on submission, where the enquiry arrives and whether a staff member can recognize the service and location. Use an approved test address and label the submission so it can be filtered out of reporting.

Repeat the journey with a plausible exception. Try an unsupported service area, a request outside opening hours or a booking category staff cannot fulfill. Agree what the page should say and what the team will do. A basic call-back process may work well for complex work where a customer needs a conversation before a booking.

Separate the contact event from its business result. A form submission may be irrelevant, duplicated or incomplete. A click to call may end before someone answers. Ask staff to mark the outcome using simple statuses such as received, relevant, responded, booked and closed. Keep contact details in the appropriate system and share only the fields needed for review.

## Days 26 to 30. Review the evidence and choose a next action

Record the initial profile and website measurements with the dates covered. Compare later periods of similar length and note holidays, hours changes, advertising or seasonal variation. Google defines profile website clicks and call-button clicks as interactions. Confirm qualified enquiries from your own records. [Google Business Profile performance metrics](https://support.google.com/business/answer/9918094)

Use this diagnostic sequence when the numbers point in different directions:

- Relevant search impressions rise while clicks stay flat. Review whether the page title and visible offer answer the same question. Examine the actual query set before changing the page.
- People visit but call with basic questions. Ask staff which questions recur and improve the answer closest to the offer.
- Enquiries rise but bookings stay flat. Review service fit, response time and follow-up ownership.
- One town generates a substantial number of suitable requests. Check whether those customers need genuinely different coverage or booking details before commissioning a page.
- The dataset is small. Record the observations and continue monitoring while fixing clearly broken journeys.

### Ask an agency to quote observable work

Request the baseline date, exact pages and profile work, who approves business facts, the planned enquiry test and the follow-up report. Ask what the business will need to maintain. A monthly fee should have a clear relationship to work such as maintaining facts, publishing useful pages, monitoring issues or reviewing lead quality.

## Turn the first month into a scope the team can repeat

### Give each lead source an honest label

If your analytics setup and profile settings support it, agree on a consistent campaign tag for the profile's website link. Follow the tagged address on a phone and confirm it still opens the intended page. Review reporting with whoever manages analytics and record whether the tag appears in the expected report. If the site strips the query parameters on redirect, fix the routing or choose a tracking method your setup supports.

Record calls and website forms separately. The team answering calls can ask how the customer found the business when that question fits the conversation. Use an unknown category when the source is unclear. Someone can visit from Search, remember the business name and later call through Maps. Give the team room to record that uncertainty so the report supports decisions without pretending to reconstruct every step.

In a spreadsheet, put the date, requested service, broad area, channel and next action on one row. Keep private details in the approved customer system. Agree which data the business can collect and how long it needs it. Review a handful of records together before turning the sheet into a permanent reporting requirement.

### Design for two customer journeys that share a service

Imagine a visitor from a nearby town who wants to take a bike to the workshop on Saturday. They need travel and opening information, and perhaps a way to check whether a slot is available. A customer already in the neighborhood might be deciding whether to book a more involved repair online. Both can use the same service page if it covers the shop's opening arrangements and makes the booking route clear.

Sketch each journey on paper with the team. Put the likely starting page in the first column, the unanswered question in the second and the intended action in the third. Keep the initial scope to a pair of journeys staff can verify. If the business later opens a real second branch, run this exercise with that branch's availability, location and booking arrangements before adding its page.

Write a short acceptance statement for the new page. For example, a visitor should be able to find the workshop's opening hours and submit an eligible repair request in a single visit. The person answering the form should be able to identify which request was made and follow the approved next step. Those checks work for the owner, the writer and the developer.

### Make a geographic expansion decision from observed demand

At the end of the month, review the towns and neighborhoods people actually mention. Separate interest the business can serve from work it cannot fulfill. Talk to the staff who quote jobs. A town can generate many enquiries that look relevant in analytics and still be impractical once travel time or staffing enters the picture.

Write a decision for each potential area. A repeat cluster of eligible requests may justify a section about travel or availability on the existing service page. A distinct branch with a different set of services may justify a dedicated location page. An area with occasional requests might need a simple instruction to call and confirm coverage. Give each proposed change an owner and a reason drawn from the observations.

Before publishing, read the page as someone who needs a real answer. They should understand whether the business serves them and what to do next. Review the link to the page from the main services section, then check it on a phone and test its contact route. Assign a date to revisit the underlying service facts so the area description stays current.

### Make the next review about a decision

Bring the lead log and the revised pages to a short review. Ask whether staff are seeing better-fit requests, which customer questions remain and which changes the team can keep up with. If the evidence points to availability, fix the public hours and booking instructions. If it points to a missing explanation, revise the relevant page. If staff need support to answer enquiries consistently, include that work in the next scope.

End the review with one change, an owner and the result you expect to observe. The next month can then begin from evidence rather than a longer list of towns or a dashboard screenshot.

## A worksheet for the first service you choose

Write the service, the part of the region you can reliably serve and the first action a customer needs to take. In the next column, list the source of each important detail. The booking owner can confirm whether appointments are needed. The technician can approve what the service includes. The business owner can approve the published area and any travel fee. Put a review date beside information that changes seasonally.

For each task, ask which page or system changes and how you will know the work is correct. If staff ask for a clearer description of a turnaround estimate, the website and confirmation message might both need a review. The acceptance test could ask a colleague to find the estimate and explain the next step. If the phone number has changed, verify that the visible number, mobile call link and customer messages agree.

Give the owner a short list of deliverables they can see. The first line can identify the approved business facts. The second can identify the live service page and relevant profile destination. A third can show the completed enquiry test with the team's confirmation. A fourth can describe the reporting period and the person reviewing it. This record lets a small business see where its spending went and prevents two suppliers from assuming the other owns an important task.

Use the worksheet again if you introduce another service. Start from its buyer questions and approval requirements. A service sold by appointment may need a different page and response process from an urgent repair. Capture that difference early so the page, profile and staff all describe the same customer journey.
