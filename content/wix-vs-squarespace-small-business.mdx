## Wix or Squarespace: start with the business you need the website to support

For a small business, the useful Wix vs Squarespace decision is whether a particular build supports your customers, your budget and the person maintaining it. Shortlist both against the same brief. Ask for a demonstration of an ordinary content edit, a mobile enquiry and any required booking journey before committing.

Our recommendation is to choose the smallest complete setup that handles your current customer journey. Leave room for improvements once you know what visitors ask and where enquiries stall. Buying more features at launch is not a substitute for explaining your offer clearly.

**Research scope:** official sources were checked on November 2, 2026. This is editorial analysis, not a hands-on speed, usability or conversion benchmark. Vendor pages displayed different regional currencies during research, so this article does not present those figures as US checkout prices. Confirm the price, tax, term and included features in your own purchase flow.

If you are also considering self-hosted WordPress or a custom build, read our [Wix vs WordPress vs custom website guide](/insights/wix-wordpress-custom-website-small-business). This comparison concentrates on choosing between two hosted website options for an owner-led business.

## What the current official information establishes

[Wix's plan comparison](https://www.wix.com/plans) lists Light, Core, Business and Business Elite. Core and higher plans list payment acceptance, ecommerce, and scheduling and services. Wix notes that pricing and currency vary by location and that displayed annual subscriptions are paid in full. A plan label alone does not establish that every booking requirement is covered.

[Squarespace's current pricing page](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) lists Basic, Core, Plus and Advanced. It distinguishes features and contributor allowances by plan. Its [Acuity billing documentation](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/360034944391-Acuity-Scheduling-pricing-billing-and-invoices) says an Acuity subscription is separate from the website subscription. Treat scheduling as an explicit purchasing decision.

Wix also documents editing page titles and descriptions in its [SEO settings guidance](https://support.wix.com/en/article/adding-page-meta-tags). That is a verified capability, not evidence that a Wix website will outrank a Squarespace website. Ask the person building either site to show the search settings and page checks included in their scope.

These facts are a starting point. The remainder of this guide is our suggested buying and implementation method. It is intended to reveal practical differences in your proposed projects rather than turn feature lists into an unsupported league table.

## Compare the same job, not two different sales demonstrations

Write a short brief before requesting quotes. A useful hypothetical brief is an independent ceramics business that offers commissions, workshops and a small portfolio. This is a planning example, not a client case study. The business needs enquiries and occasional workshop bookings, and the owner wants to update photographs without arranging a redesign.

Give both proposals the same requirements: a clear homepage, separate commission and workshop pages, an about page, an enquiry route and a manageable way to publish new work. Add booking only if the business genuinely needs customers to select and pay for a place online. An enquiry form may be sufficient for a service that requires discussion first.

Include the unglamorous details. Who receives the message? What appears after submission? What happens if a workshop is full? Can the owner change a date safely? Who owns the account? These questions are more useful than comparing a polished demo with a rough draft.

## Work out what the website will actually cost

Separate the build fee from the software bill and the operating work. Your build fee should describe the pages, writing, design, setup, integrations, revisions and launch assistance included. The software bill should identify the specific subscriptions you will own. Ongoing work should explain what someone will do after launch, with a clear boundary between included support and new projects.

Use a worksheet with one row for each item. Record the initial payment, renewal basis, billing term, owner and cancellation implications. Leave a blank where a quote is needed. A blank is more honest than copying a price from another country or assuming an introductory offer will continue.

### Ask for the complete first-year payment schedule

Request the actual cash payments due, not only a monthly equivalent. If an annual subscription is shown as a monthly amount, identify the upfront commitment. Add domain renewal, business email, scheduling, any email marketing service, paid assets and the proposed support arrangement where applicable.

Then write a separate renewal-year view. Promotional discounts, initial setup fees and domain offers should not silently carry into it. Ask what happens if you stop paying the agency while keeping the website subscription. The answer should explain access and responsibilities rather than leave the business dependent on an informal arrangement.

### Keep staff time visible without inventing a financial return

Record tasks that will fall to the owner: approving text, supplying images, checking messages, updating availability and reviewing results. You do not need a made-up hourly cost to recognise that these tasks consume time. If nobody can maintain a feature, consider deferring it or including a specific support task.

For a fuller quote checklist, use our [small business website cost and scope guide](/insights/website-project-planner). The aim is a realistic commitment the business can maintain, rather than a headline that becomes several unrelated bills after purchase.

## Decide how customers should enquire or book

An enquiry and a booking are different commitments. An enquiry starts a conversation. A booking reserves a time, place or service under defined conditions. Before choosing software, describe which action the customer should take and what information the business needs before accepting it.

For the hypothetical studio, a custom commission might need photographs, dimensions and a conversation. A workshop might have a fixed date and capacity. Treating both as the same generic contact form could make administration awkward. Treating every commission as an immediately bookable product could create expectations the owner cannot meet.

Ask the builder to map the sequence in plain language: visitor chooses an offer, provides necessary information, sees confirmation, receives an appropriate next step and reaches the responsible person. Include cancellation, rescheduling and unavailable dates if they apply. Request a contact alternative for someone who cannot complete the online route.

### Use a booking demonstration with inconvenient cases

Do not stop after a successful reservation. Ask what happens when a date changes, an email address is mistyped or the owner is away. Where payment is involved, agree who checks the payment status and how disputed or incomplete bookings are handled. Confirm product support for each requirement rather than assuming a booking icon covers it.

The best fit for your business is the setup whose ordinary exceptions you understand. That can be a simple form with a clear response process or a more involved scheduler. The platform recommendation should follow the operating decision.

## Judge design by whether your customer can use the page

A design proposal should show how your actual information fits. Supply one long service title, an imperfectly cropped photograph, an extended testimonial with permission and the most complicated description you expect to publish. Ask to see them at desktop and phone widths.

This exposes issues that idealised template content can hide. Look for text that becomes difficult to scan, buttons that lose their context and images that obscure the subject after cropping. These are proposed inspection tasks, not a claim that either vendor fails them.

Review a page without explaining it aloud. Can another person say what is offered, who it is for, what they need to provide and what happens next? Ask them to describe their uncertainty rather than simply whether they like the design. A visually attractive page can still leave an important commercial question unanswered.

### Keep an agreed design system small

For a limited budget, ask for a manageable set of headings, colours, buttons, image treatments and recurring page sections. Show how an additional service will use that system. A collection of completely different layouts may create more maintenance decisions than the owner wants.

Agree which changes an editor should make and which should go back to the designer. There is nothing wrong with a boundary if it is clear before purchase. The problem is discovering the boundary when an urgent update is already due.

## Compare SEO through an acceptance checklist

Avoid the question “Which platform is best for SEO?” in isolation. Ask what the proposed build will include: descriptive page titles, clear service headings, purposeful internal links, appropriate page availability, an intentional URL structure and a working route from discovery to enquiry. Have the builder explain any requirement that needs an additional tool or service.

Assign one page to each important offer and buyer question where a separate page serves a real purpose. A small studio might explain commissions and workshops separately because the decisions differ. That is an information-design recommendation, not an instruction to manufacture many near-identical pages for keywords.

Ask to review the actual title and description proposed for each launch page. A repeated business slogan in every field tells you little about the individual offer. The page text should answer the question the title promises to address, without needing a visitor to click through several vague sections.

### What about AEO and AI search?

Treat answer-engine readiness as a content and implementation discussion. Can the page explain the service, limitations, process and next step clearly? Are factual statements supported? Does the published page match the business's real offer? Do not accept a promise of guaranteed AI citations as a platform advantage.

Use our [AEO implementation checklist](/insights/answer-engine-optimization-small-business-checklist) for a separate task-based audit. It focuses on improving a service page and checking the implementation, rather than buying a special label for the website.

## Make the editing handover part of the purchase

Ask the intended owner to perform an ordinary update during the demonstration. For the studio example, that means replacing a workshop image, changing a date, revising a service description and checking the published mobile page. Let the owner do the work while the builder observes.

Record what required assistance. Was the problem terminology, an unclear editing boundary or the way the website was assembled? A short training note may solve a problem that otherwise looks like a platform limitation. Conversely, a workflow that requires repeated specialist intervention may be unsuitable for the owner's expectations.

Have the handover include the account owner, renewal calendar, access roles, image source folder, approved copy and a simple change process. Store recovery instructions safely. The business should understand how to request support without sharing passwords in ordinary messages.

### Define support in actions

“Maintenance included” needs an explanation. Does it include changing a service price, diagnosing a form notification, adding a new page or reviewing a broken third-party connection? Who checks messages while a problem is being investigated? What is the contact route and agreed response arrangement?

Ask for examples of included and excluded work. This helps compare two proposals fairly and prevents the cheaper build from appearing cheaper simply because its support responsibilities are missing.

## Plan a possible move before it becomes urgent

Keep a business-owned record of page copy, approved photographs, important URLs and connected services. Ask what can be exported or transferred from the particular setup and what would require rebuilding. Obtain the answer for the actual editor, account and features proposed, rather than a general statement about portability.

A future move is more than copying the homepage. Your enquiry process, booking records, email connections, redirects and account ownership need separate attention. Write down who would be responsible for checking each one. This is a contingency planning exercise, not a claim that every feature can move unchanged.

Do not choose a platform only because you might leave it. Choose an affordable operating model now while preserving the information and access needed to make a later decision deliberately. Avoid making the business dependent on a supplier's personal account.

## A practical decision meeting for a limited budget

Bring the two proposals into one meeting and evaluate them against the same requirements. Mark each requirement as demonstrated, documented, unresolved or unnecessary for launch. Do not average away an unresolved requirement that the business cannot operate without.

Consider three planning situations. An owner who changes a few service details needs a different handover from a team publishing weekly campaigns. A business taking deposits needs a different demonstration from a consultancy that qualifies every enquiry by phone. A portfolio-led business needs different sample content from one explaining a technical service. These are hypothetical situations, not vendor performance claims.

If both implementations satisfy the brief, choose according to the operating commitment: who will edit, how support works and what the complete recurring cost will be. If neither does, simplify the brief or explore a different approach before spending money on a compromise you already know will be difficult.

### What to defer

Put optional features in a later-phase list with a trigger for reconsideration. Examples might include a more involved booking flow, an additional publishing format or an integration that nobody currently needs. The trigger should be a real operating need, not a vague ambition to look bigger.

Keep the first release complete within its agreed scope. Deferring an unnecessary feature is sensible. Launching a contact form without someone responsible for answering it is an unfinished customer journey.

## Turn the platform choice into a usable website brief

Before approving the project, write a one-page decision record. Include why you chose the implementation, the customer action it supports, who will maintain it and which requirements were deferred. Attach the demonstrated workflows and payment schedule. This becomes a reference when a new request appears after launch.

Give the business owner an explicit final review: factual accuracy, service availability, image permissions and contact details. Give the builder a separate implementation review: published pages, navigation, forms and agreed search setup. Both reviews matter, and neither should be assumed to happen automatically because the design was approved.

### What to send with your first enquiry

Send the existing website address if there is one, the services you want to prioritise and the customer action that matters most. Include the available launch budget and the amount you can reasonably maintain afterwards. Mention whether writing, photography and ordinary updates will be handled internally.

If you already use a scheduler or email service, name it and explain what it does today. A supplier can then evaluate whether keeping it would reduce unnecessary change. This gives the first discussion a concrete starting point and helps avoid buying a replacement before understanding the existing work.
